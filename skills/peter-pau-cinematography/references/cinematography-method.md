# 鲍德熹式摄影、光影与构图方法论

This reference distills public, observable principles from Peter Pau / 鲍德熹's interviews, production reports, and major cinematography work. Use it as a practical visual design toolkit.

## 1. Core Thesis

The usable core is:

**故事情绪先行；用构图、光线、运动、色彩和镜头距离把文字形象化。**

Do not start from "beautiful composition." Start from what the scene means, what the director wants, and what emotional force the image must carry.

## 2. Director-DP Relationship

Treat cinematography as the director's visual thinking partner:

- Ask the director what the scene is really about, especially when the script gives only physical action.
- Find the overlap between the script, the director's intention, and your own image reading.
- Resolve contradictions early. If the emotional premise is unclear, you may choose the wrong light, composition, camera movement, or lens.
- Suggest visual ideas only when they help the story; decorative beauty can be ignored.

Practical question set:

```text
What is the scene's hidden emotion?
What does the director not say directly but wants the audience to feel?
What should the audience understand without dialogue?
Which image lever carries that meaning: light, frame, movement, color, distance, or environment?
```

## 3. Watercolor Realism

For restrained dramatic work, use a "Chinese watercolor" discipline:

- Low contrast, softened light, desaturated colors.
- Avoid extreme blacks, pure whites, heavy fog, and saturated red/blue if they would overdramatize the story.
- Let production design and color timing carry the color arc instead of forcing every scene with lighting effects.
- Build a narrative color progression: normal world, passionate memory, tragic/uncertain resolution.

Use this when the story needs elegance, restraint, melancholy, or emotional realism.

## 4. Light As Emotion

Light must describe feeling, not merely expose faces.

Patterns:

- **Large soft source**: use for natural, elegant, emotionally comfortable drama.
- **Mild moonlight / restrained fill**: preserve period atmosphere without making action look artificial.
- **Hard theatrical light**: use when a character's inner world needs to fracture, sharpen, or become unstable.
- **Reflected object light**: angle soft sources so swords, props, wet surfaces, or costumes catch drama without separate "beauty lighting."
- **Production-design light**: coordinate with art direction so architecture, windows, lanterns, screens, fabrics, and painted backdrops justify the light.

Rule: if a close-up can be replaced by light, movement, or environment, try the image solution first.

## 5. Composition

Frame emotional relations, not only faces.

Use:

- Foreground movement to show inner turbulence.
- Negative space to show restraint, distance, loneliness, or withheld desire.
- Human scale against landscape or architecture to create poetic smallness.
- Layered foreground/midground/background for period worlds and social pressure.
- Symmetry when ritual, order, or destiny matters; asymmetry when desire or conflict breaks order.

For a scene like a bamboo fight, the moving environment can become the emotional foreground. Do not rush to faces if the space can express the characters' inner movement.

## 6. Camera Movement

Choose movement by emotional grammar:

- **Still as water**: drama, withheld emotion, ritual, moral pressure.
- **Move like a rabbit**: action, pursuit, sudden release, kinetic discovery.
- **Floating camera**: wuxia grace, dreamlike bodies, characters moving beyond ordinary gravity.
- **Sudden push**: mental chaos, romantic disturbance, shock, or subjective pressure.
- **Camera-actor dance**: action choreography should be designed with the camera path, not merely covered from every angle.

Avoid generic handheld if the scene needs grace. If the action is about elegance, make the camera and actors perform a controlled dance.

## 7. Lens And Proximity

Lens choice should serve body dynamics and spatial clarity:

- Move closer for body dynamism in action and dance.
- Preserve background detail when the environment carries story.
- Avoid shallow telephoto flattening if it removes spatial information the audience needs.
- Prefer a format/lens package that supports the shoot's physical demands, budget, and post path.

Do not separate gear from aesthetics. The format decision is also a story and production decision.

## 8. Wuxia / Action Photography

Action is not only speed. It is spatial poetry, rhythm, and emotional pressure.

Approach:

- Design from the key visual idea: floating, pursuit, concealment, seduction, duel, ritual, or release.
- Let camera placement enter the action instead of only observing it.
- Use cranes, remote heads, or stable rigs when the camera must fly with performers.
- Use digital cleanup or post solutions when traditional smoke/hard light tricks would damage the intended visual style.
- Keep action legible from a designed angle; too many cameras can dilute choreography if the movement was built for one poetic axis.

For swords and props, look for reflection angles. A blade can shine from a large source if camera/source/object align.

## 9. Music, Dance, And Rhythm

In dance or song-driven scenes, make choreography cinematic:

- Adapt dance to camera movement, not only record staged choreography.
- Use pushes, hard light, frame compression, and corridor/architecture to physicalize inner disorder.
- Treat rhythm as blocking: camera timing, actor timing, lighting accent, cut point.

The image should make the body and emotion feel three-dimensional.

## 10. Technical Pragmatism

Be poetic, but solve production reality:

- Build rigs around location limits, performer clearance, wire work, and camera balance.
- Decide which problem belongs to lighting, art department, rigging, VFX, color timing, or editing.
- Use post not as a crutch, but to preserve the chosen visual style when practical tricks would betray it.
- Respect crew safety, rest, food, and time. A good visual plan includes industrial discipline.

## 11. Freshness Check

Before finalizing a visual plan, test:

- Does the image reveal story emotion, or only look expensive?
- Can the audience read the spatial relationship?
- Is the lighting motivated by world, emotion, or art direction?
- Is the camera movement emotionally necessary?
- Does the color strategy evolve with the narrative?
- Are VFX, rigging, and safety needs realistic?
- Have crew time and budget shaped a better design instead of a weaker compromise?
